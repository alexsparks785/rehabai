# Patellar Tendinitis Exercises: Complete Jumper's Knee Recovery Guide

Patellar tendinitis—commonly called jumper's knee—affects the tendon connecting your kneecap to your shinbone. This condition plagues athletes in jumping sports like basketball, volleyball, and track, but can affect anyone who loads their knees repetitively.

The good news: patellar tendinopathy responds exceptionally well to specific loading exercises. Research shows that progressive tendon loading produces better outcomes than rest, injections, or surgery for most cases.

## Understanding Patellar Tendinopathy

### What's Actually Happening

The patellar tendon connects the kneecap (patella) to the tibia (shinbone), transferring force from your quadriceps to straighten your knee. In patellar tendinopathy:

- Tendon structure becomes disorganized
- Failed healing response occurs
- Tendon may thicken
- Pain develops with loading

**Important:** Despite the "-itis" suffix suggesting inflammation, chronic patellar tendon pain is usually tendinosis (degeneration), not tendinitis (inflammation). This is why loading exercises work better than anti-inflammatory treatments.

### Stages of Tendinopathy

**Reactive Tendinopathy (Early)**
- Acute response to sudden overload
- Tendon thickens to handle load
- More reversible
- Responds well to load modification

**Tendon Dysrepair (Intermediate)**
- Attempted healing with structural changes
- Some reversibility with proper loading
- Most common presentation

**Degenerative Tendinopathy (Advanced)**
- Significant structural changes
- Areas of cell death
- Still responds to exercise
- May have longer recovery

### Causes

- Sudden increase in jumping/landing volume
- Rapid increase in training intensity
- Inadequate recovery between sessions
- Hard playing surfaces
- Poor landing mechanics
- Quad or hip weakness
- Stiff ankles

### Symptoms

- Pain at the bottom of the kneecap
- Pain with jumping, landing, running
- Pain with stairs (especially down)
- Pain with prolonged sitting
- Stiffness after rest that "warms up"
- Tenderness to touch at tendon
- Possible swelling or thickening

## Why Loading Works

The research is clear: progressive loading is the most effective treatment for patellar tendinopathy.

**Loading exercises work by:**
1. Stimulating healthy tendon adaptation
2. Improving collagen organization
3. Building tendon strength and stiffness
4. Reducing pain through neurological changes
5. Increasing load tolerance

**What doesn't work as well:**
- Complete rest (leads to further weakening)
- Anti-inflammatory medications (masks symptoms)
- Stretching alone (doesn't build tendon capacity)
- Cortisone injections (may impair healing)

## Phase 1: Pain Management with Isometrics (Weeks 1-2)

Isometric exercises reduce pain and maintain tendon capacity without the stress of movement.

### Isometric Leg Extension

**Seated Isometric Quad Hold**
1. Sit on chair or bench with knee bent to 60°
2. Extend knee against immovable resistance (wall, heavy furniture)
3. Hold at 70% maximum effort
4. Hold 45 seconds
5. 5 repetitions
6. 3 times daily
7. Pain during exercise should be ≤3/10

**Spanish Squat Isometric (Excellent for Patellar Tendon)**
1. Loop band around fixed object at knee height
2. Place band behind both knees
3. Lean back into band, feet forward
4. Hold squat position at 70-90° knee bend
5. Hold 45 seconds
6. 4-5 repetitions
7. Band allows you to load tendon with less quad demand

### Activity Modification

**Reduce but don't eliminate:**
- Jumping and landing
- Running
- Deep squatting
- Stair running

**Maintain:**
- Walking
- Swimming
- Cycling (adjust seat height)
- Upper body training

## Phase 2: Eccentric Loading (Weeks 2-8)

Eccentric exercises—where the muscle lengthens under load—are the gold standard for tendon rehabilitation.

### Decline Squat Protocol

The decline board squat is the most researched exercise for patellar tendinopathy.

**Equipment needed:** 25° decline board (or wedge, or heels on weight plates)

**Single-Leg Decline Squat (Standard Protocol)**
1. Stand on decline board, affected leg only
2. Slowly lower over 3-4 seconds (eccentric phase)
3. Stop at 60-70° knee flexion
4. Use other leg or hands to help return to standing
5. Repeat eccentric lowering on affected leg

**Protocol:**
- 3 sets of 15 repetitions
- Twice daily
- 7 days per week
- 12 weeks minimum

**Progression:**
- Start with body weight
- Add weight via backpack or holding dumbbells
- Increase load when current level produces <3/10 pain
- Can progress to 30+ kg added weight

**Why the decline board?**
- Increases load on patellar tendon specifically
- Reduces calf contribution to the movement
- Allows greater tendon loading at given knee angle

### Alternative: Flat Ground Eccentric Squat

If no decline board available:

1. Single-leg squat, slow lowering (3-4 seconds)
2. Use other leg to return to standing
3. Same sets/reps as decline protocol
4. Less specific but still effective

### Heavy Slow Resistance (HSR) Alternative

Some research shows Heavy Slow Resistance training is equally effective and may be preferred by some patients.

**HSR Protocol:**
- Leg press, hack squat, or squat
- 3-4 sets of 6-8 reps
- Slow tempo: 3 seconds down, 3 seconds up
- 3 times per week
- Heavy weight (manage pain 3-5/10)
- 12 weeks

## Phase 3: Strength Development (Weeks 6-12)

As pain decreases, build overall leg strength.

### Quadriceps Strengthening

**Leg Press**
1. Moderate to heavy load
2. 3 sets of 10-12 reps
3. Full range as tolerated
4. Control both directions

**Leg Extension (Full Range)**
1. Machine leg extension
2. 3 sets of 12-15 reps
3. Control the lowering phase
4. May need to avoid last 30° of extension if painful

**Bulgarian Split Squat**
1. Rear foot elevated
2. Front foot forward
3. Lower with control
4. 3 sets of 10 each leg

**Step-Ups**
1. Moderate height box
2. Drive through top leg
3. Lower with control
4. 3 sets of 12 each leg

### Hip Strengthening

Hip weakness contributes to knee loading patterns.

**Hip Thrust**
1. Upper back on bench
2. Drive hips up by squeezing glutes
3. 3 sets of 12-15
4. Add weight progressively

**Side-Lying Hip Abduction**
1. Lie on side
2. Raise top leg toward ceiling
3. 3 sets of 15-20 each side

**Monster Walks**
1. Band around ankles
2. Walk sideways in squat position
3. 3 sets of 15 steps each direction

### Calf Strengthening

**Calf Raises**
1. Standing or seated
2. 3 sets of 15-20
3. Full range of motion
4. Progress to single leg

## Phase 4: Power and Return to Sport (Weeks 10-16+)

### Energy Storage Loading

Before returning to jumping, the tendon needs to handle energy storage (stretch-shortening cycle).

**Jump Squats (Low Height)**
1. Quarter squat, explode upward
2. Soft landing
3. 3 sets of 8-10
4. Pain should stay ≤2/10

**Box Jumps (Low)**
1. Jump onto low box
2. Step down (don't jump down initially)
3. 3 sets of 8
4. Progress height gradually

**Lateral Bounds**
1. Single-leg hop side to side
2. Stick landing 2 seconds
3. 3 sets of 6 each direction

### Plyometric Progression

**Level 1: Bilateral Landing**
- Jump and land on both feet
- Focus on soft, controlled landing
- Low height, low volume

**Level 2: Bilateral Jumping**
- Continuous jumping
- Still bilateral
- Progress height and volume

**Level 3: Single-Leg Landing**
- Hop and stick landing
- Control is key
- Start low

**Level 4: Single-Leg Continuous**
- Single-leg hops in place
- Progress to forward/lateral movement

**Level 5: Sport-Specific**
- Sport-related jumping patterns
- Cutting and direction change
- Full intensity last

### Return to Sport Criteria

**Before full return:**
- Pain ≤2/10 with all training
- Single-leg decline squat: 25+ reps pain-free
- Single-leg hop: 10+ consecutive without pain
- Plyometric progression completed
- Sport-specific drills without symptom increase
- Typically 3-6 months from start

## Sample Weekly Programs

### Weeks 1-2: Isometric Phase
**3× daily:**
- Isometric quad holds: 5 × 45 sec
- Spanish squat isometric: 5 × 45 sec (if tolerated)

**Daily:**
- Ice after activity if needed
- Activity modification

### Weeks 2-8: Eccentric Phase
**Twice daily:**
- Decline single-leg squats: 3 × 15

**3× weekly:**
- Begin general strengthening
- Hip exercises
- Maintain cardio (non-painful)

### Weeks 6-12: Strengthening Phase
**4× weekly:**
- Continue decline squats: once daily
- Leg press: 3 × 10-12
- Bulgarian split squat: 3 × 10
- Hip strengthening circuit

### Weeks 10+: Return to Sport Phase
**3× weekly:**
- Plyometric progression
- Sport-specific drills

**2× weekly:**
- Maintenance strengthening

## Pain Monitoring

### The 24-Hour Rule

Some pain during exercise is acceptable, but:
- Pain should not exceed 5/10 during exercise
- Pain should return to baseline within 24 hours
- Morning stiffness should not significantly increase
- If pain increases day-to-day, reduce load

### When to Progress

Move to next phase when:
- Current exercises produce <3/10 pain
- Pain returns to baseline within 24 hours
- You've completed minimum time in phase
- Function has improved

## Common Mistakes

### Training Errors

**Resting completely:**
- Tendons weaken without load
- Relative rest, not complete rest

**Returning too quickly:**
- Tendons take 3-6 months to remodel
- Feeling better ≠ being healed

**Inconsistent loading:**
- Daily exercises required
- Sporadic training doesn't work

### Exercise Errors

**Going too fast:**
- Slow eccentrics are key
- 3-4 seconds lowering

**Using too light a load:**
- Tendons need heavy loads to adapt
- Progressive overload required

**Avoiding pain entirely:**
- Pain of 3-5/10 is acceptable during exercise
- Complete avoidance means inadequate loading

## Adjunct Treatments

### Useful Adjuncts

**Patellar tendon strap:**
- May reduce pain during activity
- Useful during return to sport
- Not a substitute for exercises

**Shockwave therapy:**
- Emerging evidence for chronic cases
- Typically 3-5 sessions
- Use with exercise program

### Less Useful

**Cortisone injections:**
- May impair tendon healing
- Short-term relief only
- Generally avoided

**PRP injections:**
- Mixed evidence
- Expensive
- Exercise still required regardless

## When to Seek Help

**See a healthcare provider if:**
- Severe pain limiting walking
- Swelling that doesn't resolve
- No improvement after 6-8 weeks of exercise
- Complete tendon rupture signs (sudden pop, inability to straighten knee)

## Key Takeaways

1. **Patellar tendinopathy is tendinosis** — needs loading, not rest
2. **Isometrics first** — for pain relief (5 × 45 sec holds)
3. **Decline eccentric squats are gold standard** — 3 × 15, twice daily
4. **12 weeks minimum** — tendons take time to remodel
5. **Pain of 3-5/10 during exercise is acceptable** — not zero
6. **Progressive overload** — add weight as you improve
7. **Complete return takes 3-6 months** — be patient
8. **Plyometrics before sport** — energy storage capacity matters

Jumper's knee can be frustrating, especially for athletes eager to return to their sport. But the research is clear: progressive loading exercises work. Commit to the program, be patient, and trust the process. Most athletes return to full sport with proper rehabilitation.
